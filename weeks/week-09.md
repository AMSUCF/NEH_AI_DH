The second Code Web workshop is a deliberate counterweight to the first. Workshop 4 was about deploying something professional and useful; Workshop 5 is about play, accessibility, copyright, and the AI policy that goes around your assignments. We build a small playful tool — a recommender, a generator, a quiz, a memorial — and use the second half of the session to draft a course AI policy through the lens of Universal Design for Learning.

## Session Outline (120 minutes)

1. **Why playful (~10 min).** Refik Anadol does it. Mark Sample does it. Allison Parrish does it. Play makes the labor of iteration visible and survivable. Reading anchor: any of the *Critical Making* exercises in `CriticalMaking2026/`.
2. **Live build: a memorial Artifact (~25 min).** I build something small and a little strange — a memorial, a generator, a recommender for an obscure corpus. The point is to model how to take a humanities-scale dataset and make it interactive without scope-creeping into a "real" application.
3. **Hands-on: build your tool (~40 min).** Each participant builds a small interactive tool relevant to their teaching or research. Examples to choose from:
   - A clickable timeline for a course unit
   - A recommender that takes a few user inputs and returns a curated suggestion (a book, an artwork, a primary source)
   - A quiz or knowledge-check tool with results that explain rather than just grade
   - A generator (a Markov-chain mash-up of public-domain text, a centosizer for poetry, a found-poetry tool)
4. **AI policy drafting (~30 min).** Pivot to the syllabus question. Working in pairs, draft (or revise) a one-page course AI policy that addresses:
   - **Copyright and attribution.** What gets cited, what gets attributed, what gets refused.
   - **Accessibility.** Including AI as a tool *for* accessibility (alt-text, captioning, plain-language translation), not just a problem.
   - **Equity of access.** Who pays for Pro? Are alternatives provided?
   - **Labor.** Whose labor is being replaced or extended?
   - **UDL.** Multiple means of engagement, representation, and action/expression.
5. **Discussion (~15 min).** Read each other's policies. What surprised you? What would your colleagues push back on?

## Core Exercise

Two halves, both required:

**Part A — Build a small playful tool.** Use Claude Code Web to build an interactive teaching tool relevant to your discipline. Deploy via GitHub Pages. Prioritize delight, weirdness, and clarity over scale — small is good. *Source: `HumanitiesAI/weeknine.md` and `weektwelve.md`; `CriticalMaking2026/exercises/`.*

**Part B — Draft a course AI policy.** Write a one-page AI policy for a course you teach (or plan to teach). Address copyright, attribution, accessibility, equity of access, labor, and UDL. Frame the policy as an invitation, not a prohibition — what does ethical use *enable*, not just what does it forbid?

## Pedagogical Note

The most common mistake in AI policies is treating them as a fence. The students who most need clear AI guidance are the ones least likely to ask — the ones who already feel anxious about what counts as cheating. A policy framed as *invitation* (here is when AI use is welcome, here is how to attribute it, here is what to do if you are unsure) reaches those students. A policy framed as *prohibition* drives them underground.
